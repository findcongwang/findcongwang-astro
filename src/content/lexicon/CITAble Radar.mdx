Most strategy work conflates tactics with strategy. Organisations produce detailed plans for what they will do without first establishing why it matters or who it serves. The result is strategic activity disconnected from any coherent understanding of the problem being addressed. The failure is structural.

**CITAble Radar** renders strategic structure inside-out: from a contextual understanding of a problem, outward through success criteria, concrete tactics, and observable artifacts. Visualised as concentric rings radiating from a central problem context, with sectors represented as angular arcs sharing that core, the map makes strategic structure legible across multiple parties simultaneously. The framework originated from applying the journalist's fundamental inquiry (the five Ws and one H) to strategic planning. The contextual core asks: what is the problem? Why does it matter? Who cares? From these questions, everything else follows.

What makes the CITAble Radar distinct is its problem-first core that keeps strategy grounded in context rather than aspiration. It supports bidirectional traversal and incorporates temporal evolution:

- **Problem-first core**: keeps strategy grounded in context rather than aspiration
- **Bidirectional traversal**: build inside-out or diagnose outside-in
- **Temporal evolution**: reveals how strategy changes across time

Predecessors like the Balanced Scorecard introduced multiple perspectives but remained metric-driven and static. Mintzberg distinguished deliberate from emergent strategy but provided no visual architecture. The CITAble Radar addresses these omissions by rooting everything in problem understanding and making the layer transitions explicit and navigable.

## The Four Content Rings

The Radar renders the four content bands of a [CITAble business](/lexicon/citable-businesses) (Context, Indicators, Tactics, Artifacts) as concentric rings, with three thin decorative bands (Brand, Legal, Ecosystem) as annotations around the edge.

### Context Ring: What, Why, Who Cares

This is the gravitational centre. It begins with the 5W1H inquiry applied to a problem space:

- **What is it?** Define the problem domain precisely. What phenomenon are we addressing? This requires describing symptoms, patterns, and boundaries without jumping to causes or solutions.
- **Why does it matter?** Evidence of impact, urgency, scale. What happens if nothing changes? This layer requires quantifying or qualitatively documenting consequences of inaction.
- **Who cares?** Stakeholders affected, audiences served, interests at play. This includes both direct and indirect parties whose well-being or goals are influenced by the problem and potential responses.

The Context Ring stays entirely in the problem space. No solutioning occurs here. This is what Problem-First Research produces at depth: a rich understanding of a problem that grounds everything downstream. The ring should capture complexity without confusion: multiple perspectives, historical roots, interconnections with other problems, and evidence of persistence or escalation.

The Context Ring provides the gravitational centre from which all other rings emanate. Without it, strategy floats free of reality. With it, every tactical choice can be traced back to a specific understanding of what matters and for whom. When a new initiative enters your organisation, ask first: where is its Context? If none exists or if it is merely an assumption, the initiative lacks grounding.

### Indicators Ring: Strategy, Metrics, Parameters, Success Criteria

From the contextual understanding, we derive strategic direction and measurements of success. This ring answers: given our problem understanding, what direction do we take? How will we know we are making progress? What are the parameters and constraints? What does good look like?

This is the Indicators (Strategy) band in the CITAble framework. Strategy lives here because placing it in Context would contaminate the problem-first diagnostic space with solutioning. The Indicators Ring includes:

- **Success metrics**: quantifiable indicators, thresholds, benchmarks
- **Parameters**: boundaries of acceptable action, trade-offs between competing values, rules of engagement
- **Strategic priorities**: which aspects of the problem receive focus first, sequencing decisions, resource allocation guidelines

The Indicators Ring changes infrequently and deliberately. When it changes often, either the context understanding was shallow or the metrics were poorly derived. Stability here provides the reference frame against which faster-moving rings are evaluated. A strategy that shifts with every new data point indicates metric instability or context volatility. A strategy that never shifts despite changing circumstances indicates inflexibility or fossilisation.

The Indicators Ring is dual-sided. Beyond desired outcomes, it tracks derailment signals: the constraints and avoidances that indicate strategy is going wrong. This mirrors the structure of a [Compelling Question](/lexicon/compelling-question), which fuses bold ambition with significant constraints. Desired metrics answer "how do we know we are succeeding?" Derailment metrics answer "how do we know we are failing before it is too late?" In visualisation, these two sides can be colour-coded on the same ring, making both aspirational direction and protective boundaries simultaneously visible. The Indicators Ring functions as both compass (where to go) and guardrail (where not to go).

### Tactics Ring: Who, Where, How

From the strategy, arise specifics. The remaining Ws and the H find their home here:

- **Who** will do the work? (actors, teams, partners, decision rights, accountability structures)
- **Where** will it happen? (domains, geographies, platforms, physical or digital spaces, coordination mechanisms)
- **How** will it be accomplished? (methods, tools, processes, routines, capabilities required, decision protocols)

Tactics are concrete, observable, and adaptive. They respond to shifts in context while maintaining alignment with strategic parameters. Tactics should be traceable to specific strategic metrics. When a tactic cannot be traced back to a specific strategic metric, it is either misplaced or reveals a gap in the strategy ring.

Effective tactics exhibit three qualities:

- **Implementable**: realistic given available resources and constraints
- **Measurable**: their contribution to strategic metrics can be observed
- **Reversible**: allowing for learning and course correction

Complex tactics often break down into sequences or combinations of simpler ones. The Tactics Ring captures the rhythm of execution.

### Artifacts Ring: All Work, Curated Proof Highlighted

Artifacts are directly caused by tactics. The outer ring holds all work at any progress stage: active, in-progress, completed, shelved. Within the ring, **curated public-proof artifacts are highlighted**: the selected subset chosen for public visibility and social proof. The ring includes:

- Writings (reports, publications, content, code, documentation)
- Projects (initiatives, products, services, infrastructure)
- Real-life case studies (documented outcomes, implementation records, impact assessments)
- Hypothetical future scenarios (design fiction, speculative outputs, anticipatory prototypes)

Highlighted artifacts serve a dual function: they demonstrate execution and they provide feedback. When artifacts consistently fail to produce expected outcomes, the signal propagates inward: either tactics are misaligned, strategy metrics are wrong, or the contextual understanding was incomplete.

The Artifacts Ring is where strategy becomes visible to external audiences. Curated outputs may circulate independently of their origin, so clarity in tracing artifacts back to their generating tactics and underlying strategy matters for credibility and learning. A single piece of work may serve multiple purposes; when highlighted as public proof, its full lineage through all four content rings becomes publicly navigable.

## The Decorative Bands: Brand, Legal, Ecosystem

Around the four content rings sit three thin decorative bands. They do not hold the strategic through-line; they annotate it.

- **Brand** explains how an item is communicated (voice, messaging, positioning).
- **Legal** records the private-internal entity, contract, and IP detail behind the work. It is kept internal.
- **Ecosystem** maps the network of people, organisations, and roles around an item: coalition partners, connections, and competitive players.

These bands provide the enabling context for items on the Radar without competing with the Context-to-Artifacts strategic spine.

## Bidirectional Traversal

The CITAble Radar supports two modes of use:

Design mode (inside-out): Start from a problem understanding (Context), derive success criteria (Strategy), determine concrete actions (Tactics), and specify expected outputs (Artifacts). This is how you build a new strategy from scratch.

Diagnosis mode (outside-in): Start from observable artifacts and reverse-engineer backward. What tactics produced these artifacts? What strategic objectives do those tactics imply? What problem understanding (or lack thereof) sits beneath?

Diagnosis mode is particularly revealing for organisations whose stated strategy contradicts their observable behaviour. By mapping artifacts backward through tactics to inferred strategy, gaps between rhetoric and reality become structural rather than anecdotal.

For example, the World Economic Forum states a strategy of stakeholder capitalism and inclusive growth. Artifact analysis (closed-door member meetings, selective invitation policies, partnership structures favouring large multinationals) reveals tactics optimised for elite network maintenance. Reverse-engineering through the Radar: the inferred Context assumption is that global governance requires elite coordination, not broad participation. The gap between stated and inferred context is where strategic incoherence lives. The framework makes this visible and precise rather than merely rhetorical.

Bidirectional traversal also supports learning across time. When a strategy succeeds, reverse-engineering explains why. When it fails, reverse-engineering identifies where the breakdown occurred. This turn-taking (design, execute, diagnose, redesign) creates a feedback loop for strategic development.

## Temporal Dimension

The four rings do not exist as a static snapshot. They evolve, and understanding that evolution enriches the tool considerably.

The four rings evolve at different paces:

- **Different paces**: Context shifts continuously; Strategy shifts rarely and deliberately; Tactics adapt responsively; Artifacts reflect execution rhythm.

- **Linear time**: Mapping how the four rings have changed historically reveals patterns. Which context shifts prompted strategic pivots? Which tactical changes produced (or failed to produce) the expected artifacts? The historical record of a CITAble Radar is itself a diagnostic tool. Comparing the Context of an organisation at two points in time reveals whether assumptions were tested and revised or merely repeated. Comparing artifacts across periods reveals whether output volume correlates with strategy adherence or merely activity.

- **Expansive time**: The framework extends into alternative scenarios. What if a specific historical context assumption had been different? What artifacts would have resulted? Projecting forward: under different future context scenarios, which strategic metrics remain valid and which require revision? This connects the CITAble Radar to scenario planning and foresight methodology, where the contextual core is stress-tested against multiple possible futures rather than a single expected one.

The temporal dimension transforms the CITAble Radar from a planning document into a living instrument for understanding how strategy actually evolves. It acknowledges that strategy is an ongoing process of adaptation governed by the relative stability and volatility of each ring.

## Applied Implementation: CITAble™ Businesses

The CITAble Radar finds its operational counterpart in the [CITAble Businesses](/lexicon/citable-businesses) framework. Where the Radar provides the visualisation and navigation system, CITAble provides the data structure and operational architecture across seven bands.

The four rings of the CITAble Radar correspond directly to the four content bands:

| Radar Ring | CITAble Band | Folder | Function |
|-------------|-------------|--------|----------|
| Context (innermost) | Context | `context/` | Problem definition: what, why, who is affected. No solutioning. |
| Indicators (second ring) | Indicators (Strategy) | `indicators-strategy/` | Strategic direction + derived metrics and success criteria |
| Tactics (third ring) | Tactics | `tactics/` | When, where, how of active execution |
| Artifacts (outer ring) | Artifacts | `artifacts/` | All work at any stage. **Curated public proof highlighted within.** |

The three decorative bands (Brand, Legal, Ecosystem) serve as annotations and background context attached to items on the Radar. Brand metadata explains how an item is communicated. Legal records the entity detail behind it. Ecosystem maps the network around it.

A CITAble business maintains its seven-band structure as living documentation. The CITAble Radar renders the four content bands as a navigable, temporal visualisation. Together, they provide both the operational backbone (CITAble) and the exploration interface (CITAble Radar) for organisations pursuing structural clarity.

## Cross-Sector Coordination

The CITAble Radar is circular in visualisation. The Context core sits at the centre (the bullseye), with Indicators, Tactics, and Artifacts as concentric rings radiating outward. Sectors are defined as angular arcs on this circle: distinct disciplines, interests, or organisations each occupying their own slice of the map.

The key structural property: multiple sectors can share the same Context core. When several parties organise around the same problem (the same "what, why, who cares"), their strategies, tactics, and artifacts radiate outward from the same gravitational centre. They need not agree on method. They need only share the problem understanding at the centre.

This creates a **coalition model**. Shared context enables coordinated action without requiring homogenised tactics. An innovation ecosystem, a cross-sector partnership, or a multi-stakeholder initiative can map each participant as a separate arc on the same circle. Where arcs align at the Indicators ring (shared success metrics), coordination is natural. Where arcs diverge at the Tactics ring (different methods), that divergence is visible and can be evaluated: does it strengthen the overall response through diversity, or does it fragment effort?

The framework provides a way to audit strategic correlation between parties. Two organisations claiming to work on the same problem can map their respective arcs and compare: do their Indicators-ring metrics align? Do their Tactics point in compatible directions? Are their Artifacts complementary or contradictory? This turns vague "partnership" language into structural analysis.
